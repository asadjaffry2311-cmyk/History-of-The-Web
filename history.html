Key Moments in the History of the Web

The Web did not appear overnight. It grew from decades of research into networking, protocols, and the desire to connect human knowledge across machines. This timeline traces that journey — from the first computer network in 1969 to the social and mobile Web of today.

Did you know? The very first message ever sent over a computer network was just two letters: "Lo" — sent on 29 October 1969 from ARPANET at UCLA to Stanford. The system crashed before "Login" could be completed. Source: DARPA

The Full Timeline

1960s – 1970s

Networks Before the Web

Long before the Web existed, researchers were already connecting computers. The key breakthrough came on 29 October 1969, when the ARPANET sent its first message from UCLA to Stanford. The message was meant to be "Login" — but the system crashed after just two letters: "Lo."

ARPANET introduced packet switching — a radical alternative to the telephone system, where a dedicated line was held open for each call. According to MDN Web Docs, ARPANET was the direct ancestor of the modern internet.

Packet Switching ARPANET UCLA → Stanford
1980s

TCP/IP and the Birth of the Modern Internet

By the early 1980s, ARPANET had grown into a patchwork of networks that could not communicate with each other. The solution was TCP/IP, developed by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn. On 1 January 1983 — "Flag Day" — ARPANET switched entirely to TCP/IP, marking the official birth of the modern Internet.

Also in the 1980s, Paul Mockapetris invented DNS, introducing domains like .com, .org, and .edu, making the internet far more navigable for everyday users.

TCP/IP DNS Flag Day 1983
1989 – 1991
⭐ Key Milestone

Tim Berners-Lee Invents the World Wide Web

On 12 March 1989, Tim Berners-Lee submitted a proposal at CERN titled "Information Management: A Proposal." His supervisor wrote: "Vague but exciting."

By Christmas 1990, Berners-Lee had defined the three core technologies that still underpin every website today:

  • HTML — the language for writing web pages
  • HTTP — the protocol for transferring pages
  • URLs — the addressing system for every Web resource

On 6 August 1991, the first public website went live at info.cern.ch. On 30 April 1993, CERN released the Web software into the public domain — free for anyone to use forever.

HTML HTTP URL info.cern.ch
1990s

Browsers and Public Access

The Web remained a tool for scientists until April 1993, when students at the University of Illinois released Mosaic — the first browser to show images and text together. According to the National Science Foundation, within 18 months, Mosaic had over a million users, and websites grew from 130 in 1993 to over 100,000 by 1996.

In 1994, Mosaic co-creator Marc Andreessen co-founded Netscape Communications. That same year, Berners-Lee founded the W3C to maintain open web standards. By 1995, Netscape Navigator had 10 million global users.

Mosaic Browser Netscape W3C Founded
2000s – Today

The Social, Mobile, and Interactive Web

Around 2004, a new generation of the Web emerged — Web 2.0 — built on user-generated content and social platforms: Wikipedia (2001), Facebook (2004), YouTube (2005), Twitter (2006). The iPhone (2007) made the Web truly mobile. Today, according to DataReportal, over 6 billion people use the internet — most through a smartphone. The Web has become the primary infrastructure of global communication, commerce, and culture.

🔤

A Semiotic Perspective on the Web's Evolution

From a semiotic perspective, the history of the Web is not just a history of technology — it is a history of how signs circulate. In the early Web (1991–1999), documents were static: a page was an index, pointing to fixed content authored by institutions. The hyperlink was the fundamental sign — a directional reference from one text to another, structuring knowledge as a network of connections rather than a hierarchy.

With Web 2.0, the semiotic landscape shifted fundamentally. Users became producers of signs, not just consumers. Platforms like Facebook and YouTube introduced new sign systems — likes, shares, comments, hashtags — each encoding social meaning and structuring how content is seen, ranked, and interpreted. The interface itself became a sign: button placement, colour choices, and navigation menus all carry assumptions about who the user is and what they want. As Tim Berners-Lee originally envisioned, the Web was designed as a space of human communication — and semiotics gives us the tools to read that communication critically: not as neutral delivery, but as designed meaning.
